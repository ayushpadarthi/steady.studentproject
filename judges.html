Try it in one minute

  1. Open Draw. Press Demo, turn on Simulate tremor and pick ET moderate.
  2. Turn on Show the raw, shaky line. Draw a spiral or write your name. Grey is what your hand plus the tremor did; colour is what Steady drew. The top line says what Steady detected, for example "Removing a 6.4 Hz shake".
  3. In Demo, turn on Lag race and draw a fast spiral. A blue line shows what a typical stabiliser (a moving average) would draw from the same input: just as smooth, but trailing behind your pen. The card shows how far each line is from the pen, for example "Steady 3 mm, stabiliser 12 mm".
  4. Switch Steady off in the bottom bar and draw again to see the raw line.
  5. Press R (or "Replay last stroke in slow motion" in Demo) to watch a stroke again slowly.
  6. Open the Lab, draw with simulated tremor, then Compare filters on this drawing: the same input through no filter, a moving average, One-Euro and Steady, side by side, with numbers.

The problem

People with hand tremor (for example essential tremor) find it hard to draw a clean line. Drawing apps offer "stabilisers", but these average recent movement, so the line trails behind the pen. The more shake they remove, the more they lag, and a lagging line feels like drawing through syrup.

Engineering goal: remove most of the tremor while keeping the delay under 20 ms, and never make a healthy hand's drawing worse.

How Steady works

Tremor is a fast back-and-forth rhythm (about 4-12 times a second). The movement you mean is slower. Steady separates the two instead of averaging everything.

  1. 1. Pen positionabout 120 times a second
  2. 2. Rhythm detector19 oscillators from 3 to 12 Hz, tracked by Kalman filters, subtract the tremor with almost no delay
  3. 3. Gatecancels fully only when a clear tremor is there, and not when the rhythm looks like deliberate drawing
  4. 4. Line while drawinglight 10 Hz finish, 18 ms total delay
  5. 5. Settlingafter the pen lifts, the stroke is smoothed both ways (no delay), by an amount set by the measured tremor
More detail on each part

Rhythm detector. Each oscillator models a tremor at one frequency (3, 3.5, ... 12 Hz). A Kalman filter estimates how much of each is in the pen movement. Four copies with different "how fast can the tremor change" settings run side by side and are weighted by how well they predict the pen. The tremor estimate is subtracted from the position, which leaves the intended movement without averaging it.

Gate. On a healthy hand there is little rhythm to remove, and trying anyway adds error. So the cancellation fades in only when the detected tremor is larger than about 0.1-0.3 mm. We added this after real drawings showed that the ungated filter did nothing useful. Honest note: v6 reads a healthy hand's tiny tremor as a little bigger, so for a healthy hand the gate is partly on (about a quarter on average in our tests). That does not make healthy drawing worse (0.08 mm vs 0.10 mm with no filter).

Drawing check (v5) and size limit (v7). A rhythm that is slow (under about 4 Hz) and big (over about 1 mm) is far more likely a zigzag you mean to draw than tremor, so Steady leaves it alone. So is any rhythm far bigger than tremor gets while drawing (detected size above about 16 mm), whatever its speed, which keeps big colouring scribbles.

Settling. While you draw, Steady cannot see the future, so it must stay fast. After pen-up the whole stroke is known, and a zero-phase filter (run forwards and backwards) smooths it without delay. Its strength follows the tremor Steady measured, and points may move at most about twice the tremor size, so real shapes keep their size.

Is our simulated tremor realistic?

Steady predicts the tremor's rhythm, so a rhythm that changes from beat to beat is harder for it. We checked our simulator against published measurements: the tremor stability index (how much the tremor's speed changes from one beat to the next; di Biase et al., Brain, 2017) is 1.3-1.9 Hz for essential tremor. Our simulator scored 0.84-1.04 Hz, so real essential tremor is even less steady than we had assumed. We built a second, literature-matched tremor model (its target fixed before looking at any results), tested every method on both, and retuned Steady on training data to follow changing tremor better (v6).

Colouring in (the v7 fix) and its limit

Colouring in means fast back-and-forth strokes, often 4-6 per second: exactly the speed of tremor. v6 erased big scribbles like that. v7 adds a size limit: real tremor while drawing is rarely bigger than about 1.5 cm (in a clinical study, even a spiral rated 3 out of 4 had about 3.4 cm peak-to-peak; Elble and Ellenbogen, 2017), so anything bigger is treated as drawing. The limit we cannot fix: small, fast colouring strokes (about 0.5-1.5 cm at 4-6 per second) look exactly like moderate-to-severe tremor. No method that only sees the pen's position can tell them apart; a moving average erases them just as much. Our proposed next design: use your tremor size. The spiral check-in already measures how big your shake is; Steady could then only remove wiggles up to about twice that size. Someone with a 3 mm tremor would keep a 1 cm colouring stroke. Not built or tested yet.

Real drawings

On the held-out half of 40 real spiral drawings from a public dataset (UCI 395: people with Parkinson's and healthy volunteers), Steady improved accuracy by 21 % (Parkinson's) and 26 % (healthy), the same as a moving average with the same delay. The tremor in that dataset is small while drawing, so the big gains above are from simulation. There is no public dataset of drawings by people with essential tremor.

Ideas we tested and rejected: filtering along the tremor's direction (healthy hands got worse); a warm-up spiral before drawing (no gain: Steady locks on within the first stroke); starting the detector at 4 Hz instead of 3 Hz (severe tremor got 13-50 % worse); tracking the pen while it hovers above the screen (worse at stroke starts). We also decided not to feed a motion sensor into the filter: the gain would be small, and the sensor is more useful as an independent check.

How we kept the test fair

  • Criteria were fixed before testing. Data was split into a training half (for choosing settings) and a held-out half, used once at the end.
  • Every method sees exactly the same drawings. The other methods were tuned on the same training data, both without a delay limit and with Steady's 20 ms limit.
  • Disclosed: after the first held-out run of v4 we re-picked two settling thresholds on training data, because the healthy final line had got worse. The same happened with v6: its first held-out run made the healthy final line worse (0.18 mm), so the smoothing thresholds were re-picked on training data and a second, disclosed held-out run checked every design goal. Every extra look at held-out data is written down.
  • Final test on fresh data: because the original held-out data had been looked at several times, we wrote down a new, larger final test (40 new sessions per kind of tremor) before running it, ran it once, and report those numbers above. WFLC and BMFLC were then re-tuned on training data with both tremor models and wider settings (as Steady had been) and run once on the same final data; those fairer numbers are the ones shown.
  • The Python reference and the web app give the same answers to within 0.000000001 mm (automated test).

Prior art, and what is ours

Tremor-cancelling filters already exist, built for surgical tools and wearables. We do not claim to have invented tremor filtering. We designed one for drawing and benchmarked it against the established methods.

What we designed: (1) the gate, which scales cancellation by how clear the tremor is (mostly off for a healthy hand: about a quarter on average in our tests, without making it worse); (2) the drawing check, which keeps deliberate zigzags; (3) settling after pen-up, with its strength set by the measured tremor and limited to the tremor's size; (4) the benchmark: every method, the same drawings, criteria fixed in advance.

Limits (honest)

  • The large gains are in simulation. Our simulator is checked against real drawings, but we made both the simulator and the filter.
  • Which parts of the simulated tremor are sourced: how much its speed changes from beat to beat (matched to di Biase et al., 2017) and its size. Elble and Ellenbogen (2017) measured spiral tremor in essential tremor on a tablet; by their formula our presets match clinical spiral ratings of about 1 (mild), 1.5-2 (moderate) and 2.5-3 (severe). The typical patient in their study had about 2 mm peak-to-peak, closer to our mild preset, where Steady removes 58 %. Not sourced: how much the tremor's size changes from beat to beat; we found no published numbers.
  • What our error score does not show: it is the distance from the shape the hand meant to draw, so a line that is late but on the right path still scores well. That is why delay is measured and reported separately.
  • Delay numbers are the filter's own. Tablets and screens add their own delay (often quoted as tens of milliseconds; we did not measure it) to every method equally, so the felt difference is Steady's 18 ms versus a heavy stabiliser's 76 ms, on top of the device's delay.
  • The "typical stabiliser" in the lag race is a stand-in: a 2 Hz moving average. Stabilisers in drawing apps differ in how they smooth.
  • No testing with people yet. That needs approval from our school's review committee first (what a session would involve).
  • A small, fast wiggle drawn on purpose (faster than about 4 times a second and smaller than about 1.5 cm, like careful colouring in) cannot be told apart from tremor by position alone (see "Colouring in" above). Very sharp, slow zigzags can lose about 1 mm at the corners in the final line.
  • The tremor pen, a servo that shakes a stylus repeatably and measures the shake with its own motion sensor, is designed and its software tested on a computer. It is not built yet.
  • Steady is not a medical device and does not treat tremor. It changes what appears on screen.

Questions we expect

Isn't this just a smoothing filter?

A smoothing filter averages everything, which causes delay. Steady estimates the tremor rhythm and subtracts only that, so the intended movement is not averaged. The tables compare it with smoothing at the same delay.

Why simulation instead of real people?

In simulation we know the line the hand meant to draw, so we can measure error exactly, and every method sees the same drawings. We also tested on public real drawings. Testing with people needs approval, which we are following.

How do you know the simulated tremor is realistic?

We measured how irregular real tremor drawings are (how much the rhythm and size change from beat to beat) and found our first simulator far too regular. We fixed it and re-ran everything; Steady's lead over a moving average at the same delay shrank, and those smaller numbers are the ones we report.

Does it need special hardware?

No. It is a web page that works with a finger, a mouse or any stylus, on a laptop, Chromebook, tablet or phone, and keeps working offline.
