  1. Open Draw. Press Demo, turn on Simulate tremor and pick ET moderate.

The problem

People with hand tremor (for example essential tremor) find it hard to draw a clean line. Drawing apps offer "stabilisers", but these average recent movement, so the line trails behind the pen. The more shake they remove, the more they lag, and a lagging line feels like drawing through syrup.

Engineering goal: remove most of the tremor while keeping the delay under 20 ms, and never make a healthy hand's drawing worse.

How Steady works

Tremor is a fast back-and-forth rhythm (about 4-12 times a second). The movement you mean is slower. Steady separates the two instead of averaging everything.

  1. 1. Pen positionabout 120 times a second
  2. 2. Rhythm detector19 oscillators from 3 to 12 Hz, tracked by Kalman filters, subtract the tremor with almost no delay
  3. 3. Gateonly cancels when a clear tremor is there, and not when the rhythm looks like deliberate drawing
  4. 4. Line while drawinglight 10 Hz finish, 16 ms total delay
  5. 5. Settlingafter the pen lifts, the stroke is smoothed both ways (no delay), by an amount set by the measured tremor
More detail on each part

Rhythm detector. Each oscillator models a tremor at one frequency (3, 3.5, ... 12 Hz). A Kalman filter estimates how much of each is in the pen movement. Four copies with different "how fast can the tremor change" settings run side by side and are weighted by how well they predict the pen. The tremor estimate is subtracted from the position, which leaves the intended movement without averaging it.

Gate. On a healthy hand there is little rhythm to remove, and trying anyway adds error. So the cancellation fades in only when the detected tremor is larger than about 0.1-0.3 mm. We added this after real drawings showed that the ungated filter did nothing useful.

Drawing check (v5). A rhythm that is slow (under about 4 Hz) and big (over about 1 mm) is far more likely a zigzag you mean to draw than tremor, so Steady leaves it alone.

Settling. While you draw, Steady cannot see the future, so it must stay fast. After pen-up the whole stroke is known, and a zero-phase filter (run forwards and backwards) smooths it without delay. Its strength follows the tremor Steady measured, and points may move at most about twice the tremor size, so real shapes keep their size.

Real drawings

On the held-out half of 40 real spiral drawings from a public dataset (UCI 395: people with Parkinson's and healthy volunteers), Steady improved accuracy by 21 % (Parkinson's) and 26 % (healthy), the same as a moving average with the same delay. The tremor in that dataset is small while drawing, so the big gains above are from simulation. There is no public dataset of drawings by people with essential tremor.

Design iterations

VersionWhat changedWhy (what testing showed)

v3Gate + light finishOn real drawings v2 did almost nothing and could add error on healthy hands
SettlingZero-delay smoothing after pen-up, with a shake-size limitThe live line was accurate but not the smoothest; the limit came after colouring-page fill fell from 97 % to 40 %
v4Retuned on realistic tremorOur tremor simulator was too regular compared with real drawings; on the realistic one v3 missed the 50 % goal (48 %)
v5Drawing checkDeliberate zigzags were being erased

Ideas we tested and rejected: filtering along the tremor's direction (healthy hands got worse); a warm-up spiral before drawing (no gain: Steady locks on within the first stroke); starting the detector at 4 Hz instead of 3 Hz (severe tremor got 13-50 % worse); tracking the pen while it hovers above the screen (worse at stroke starts). We also decided not to feed a motion sensor into the filter: the gain would be small, and the sensor is more useful as an independent check.

How we kept the test fair

  • Criteria were fixed before testing. Data was split into a training half (for choosing settings) and a held-out half, used once at the end.
  • Every method sees exactly the same drawings. The other methods were tuned on the same training data, both without a delay limit and with Steady's 20 ms limit.
  • Disclosed: after the first held-out run of v4 we re-picked two settling thresholds on training data, because the healthy final line had got worse. Every extra look at held-out data is written down.
  • The Python reference and the web app give the same answers to within 0.000000001 mm (automated test).

Prior art, and what is ours

Tremor-cancelling filters already exist, built for surgical tools and wearables. We do not claim to have invented tremor filtering. We designed one for drawing and benchmarked it against the established methods.

What we designed: (1) the gate, which switches cancellation off without clear tremor; (2) the drawing check, which keeps deliberate zigzags; (3) settling after pen-up, with its strength set by the measured tremor and limited to the tremor's size; (4) the benchmark: every method, the same drawings, criteria fixed in advance.

Limits (honest)

  • The large gains are in simulation. Our simulator is checked against real drawings, but we made both the simulator and the filter.
  • No testing with people yet. That needs approval from our school's review committee first (what a session would involve).
  • A small, fast wiggle drawn on purpose (faster than about 4 times a second, under 1 mm) cannot be told apart from tremor by position alone. Very sharp, slow zigzags can lose about 1 mm at the corners in the final line.
  • The tremor pen, a servo that shakes a stylus repeatably and measures the shake with its own motion sensor, is designed and its software tested on a computer. It is not built yet.
  • Steady is not a medical device and does not treat tremor. It changes what appears on screen.

Questions we expect

Isn't this just a smoothing filter?

A smoothing filter averages everything, which causes delay. Steady estimates the tremor rhythm and subtracts only that, so the intended movement is not averaged. The tables compare it with smoothing at the same delay.

Why simulation instead of real people?

In simulation we know the line the hand meant to draw, so we can measure error exactly, and every method sees the same drawings. We also tested on public real drawings. Testing with people needs approval, which we are following.

How do you know the simulated tremor is realistic?

We measured how irregular real tremor drawings are (how much the rhythm and size change from beat to beat) and found our first simulator far too regular. We fixed it and re-ran everything; Steady's lead over a moving average at the same delay shrank, and those smaller numbers are the ones we report.

Does it need special hardware?

No. It is a web page that works with a finger, a mouse or any stylus, on a laptop, Chromebook, tablet or phone, and keeps working offline.
